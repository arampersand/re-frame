### 1. What is an essay? Structure (~1:20)

> So, what is an essay?
>
> An essay is a short piece of academic writing about one topic. In an essay, you give your opinion, or you explain an idea, and you support it with reasons and examples. It's not a list, and it's not a story. It's an organized text with a clear structure.
>
> An essay has three main parts: the introduction, the body, and the conclusion.
>
> **The introduction** is the first paragraph. Here you present the topic, and you write the thesis statement. The thesis statement is one sentence that says the main idea of the whole essay.
>
> **The body** is the middle part. It usually has two or three paragraphs, and each paragraph talks about one idea only. Every body paragraph starts with a topic sentence, and after that you add explanations, examples, or data to support it.
>
> **The conclusion** is the last paragraph. Here you repeat the main idea in different words, you summarize your arguments, and you finish with a final comment. This is important: you never put new information in the conclusion.
>
> There's a simple way to remember the structure. Tell them what you are going to say. Say it. And then tell them what you said.

---

### 2. Types of connectors (~2:00)

> Now let's talk about connectors.
>
> Connectors, or linking words, are words that join ideas. They show the relationship between two sentences, or between two paragraphs. Without connectors, a text sounds like a robot: short sentences, no connection. With connectors, your writing sounds natural, and it's much easier to follow.
>
> There are different types. I'm going to show you six types with examples.
>
> **The first type is addition.** We use it to add information. For example: *and*, *also*, *in addition*, and *moreover*. So I can say: "The app is fast. In addition, it's free."
>
> **The second type is contrast.** We use it to show a difference or an opposite idea. For example: *but*, *however*, *although*, and *on the other hand*. So I can say: "I studied a lot. However, the exam was difficult."
>
> **The third type is cause.** It explains the reason. For example: *because*, *since*, and *due to*. So I can say: "I was late because the bus didn't come."
>
> **The fourth type is result.** It shows the consequence. For example: *so*, *therefore*, and *as a result*. So I can say: "It was raining. Therefore, we stayed at home."
>
> **The fifth type is sequence.** We use it to put ideas in order. For example: *first*, *then*, *after that*, and *finally*.
>
> **And the sixth type is example and conclusion.** To give examples we use *for example*, *for instance*, and *such as*. And to close a text we use *in conclusion*, *to sum up*, or *in short*.
>
> So, in total, that's more than ten connectors: and, also, in addition, moreover, but, however, although, on the other hand, because, since, so, therefore, as a result, first, then, finally, for example, and in conclusion.

---

### 3. Defining vs non-defining relative clauses (~1:40)

> Finally, let's talk about relative clauses.
>
> A relative clause is a part of a sentence that gives information about a noun. We introduce it with a relative pronoun: *who* for people, *which* for things, *that* for people or things, *where* for places, and *whose* for possession.
>
> There are two types: defining and non-defining. And the difference is simple — is the information necessary, or is it extra?
>
> **A defining relative clause gives necessary information.** It tells you exactly which person or which thing we are talking about. If you remove it, the sentence doesn't make sense, or the meaning changes. And this is the key point: we don't use commas.
>
> For example: "The student who studies every day passes the exam." Here, "who studies every day" tells us *which* student. It's necessary.
>
> In defining clauses we can also use *that*, and sometimes we can even remove the pronoun. For example: "The book that I read", or just "The book I read". Both are correct.
>
> **A non-defining relative clause gives extra information.** It's information that is nice to know, but it's not necessary. If you remove it, the sentence still makes sense. And here we always use commas.
>
> For example: "My teacher, who is from Chihuahua, is very strict." We already know who my teacher is. The clause only adds extra information.
>
> There are two rules for non-defining clauses. Number one: we can't use *that*. We use *who* or *which*. And number two: we can never remove the relative pronoun.
>
> Let me compare them with one last example. Defining: "The city that I live in is very hot." Non-defining: "Chihuahua, which is in the north of Mexico, is very hot." Same idea, but different information — one is necessary, and the other one is extra.

## Chuleta para memorizar (bullets, no el guion completo)

No memorices palabra por palabra. Memoriza este esqueleto y habla alrededor de él.

**Intro** → nombre → 3 temas: essay / connectors / relative clauses

**Essay**
- short academic text, one topic, opinion + reasons + examples
- 3 partes: introduction / body / conclusion
- intro → topic + thesis statement (una oración)
- body → 2-3 párrafos, una idea cada uno, topic sentence + support
- conclusion → repetir idea, resumir, cerrar. **No new information**
- frase de cierre: *tell them what you're going to say, say it, tell them what you said*

**Connectors** → 6 tipos, cada uno con ejemplo hablado
1. Addition → and, also, in addition, moreover → "The app is fast. In addition, it's free."
2. Contrast → but, however, although, on the other hand → "I studied a lot. However, the exam was difficult."
3. Cause → because, since, due to → "I was late because the bus didn't come."
4. Result → so, therefore, as a result → "It was raining. Therefore, we stayed at home."
5. Sequence → first, then, after that, finally
6. Example / conclusion → for example, for instance, such as / in conclusion, to sum up, in short

**Relative clauses**
- pronombres: who, which, that, where, whose
- **Defining** = necesaria, sin comas, sí *that*, se puede omitir el pronombre → "The student who studies every day passes the exam."
- **Non-defining** = extra, con comas, no *that*, no se omite el pronombre → "My teacher, who is from Chihuahua, is very strict."
- comparación final: "The city that I live in is very hot." / "Chihuahua, which is in the north of Mexico, is very hot."

**Cierre** → recap de los 3 temas → thank you

## Tips de grabación

- Graba en 4 tomas separadas (intro, tema 1, tema 2, tema 3 + cierre) y las pegas. Es mucho más fácil que una sola toma perfecta.
- Pon los bullets de la chuleta en post-its **detrás de la cámara**, a la altura de los ojos. Ver 3 palabras clave no es leer; leer párrafos sí se nota.
- Si escribes los ejemplos en un pizarrón o los pones en pantalla, eso justifica que mires a un lado y te da apoyo visual sin que parezca lectura.
- Habla lento. Con este guion completo salen ~6-7 minutos; el mínimo son 5, así que tienes colchón. Si te trabas, repite la idea con otras palabras — eso suena a dominio, no a error.
- Pausa un segundo entre bloque y bloque, y di el nombre del tema en voz alta ("Now, connectors"). Ayuda al profe a ver que cubriste los 3 puntos.
